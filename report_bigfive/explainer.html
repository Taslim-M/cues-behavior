Mechanistic interpretability · Llama-3.3-70B

Reading & writing a language model's personality.

Inside a large language model, a personality trait turns out to be a single direction — a line through the model's internal activity. Find that line and you can read how extraverted or agreeable the model is being. Push along it and you can rewrite that trait. This is a field guide to how we found those directions, what they mean, and what happens when we steer.

◄ less of the trait more of the trait ►
one trait  =  one direction  =  one dial you can turn

The question

Is personality something you can find — and move — in the wiring?

Psychologists compress human personality into five dimensions: Extraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, Emotional Stability, and Openness — the Big Five. We asked whether those five dimensions live as clean, usable directions inside Llama‑3.3‑70B, and — the real prize — whether the model's own default "helpful Assistant" personality is just a particular Big Five profile, or something else entirely.

STEP 01 · THE DATA

Teach it personality with characters it already knows

We can't survey a model about its "true self," but it has read enough fiction to role-play almost anyone. So we handed it a real 50-question personality quiz (the IPIP inventory) and asked it to answer as 406 fictional characters — from Tony Soprano to Leslie Knope. Each character's answers become a personality score; the model's written explanations become a rich description we can feed back in.

STEP 02 · THE PROBE

Find the one direction that predicts a trait

As the model reads each character description, we record its internal activations — 8,192 numbers at each of 80 layers. Then we ask a simple question: is there a single direction in that 8,192-dimensional space along which characters line up from low to high on a trait? Fitting one (with ridge regression) gives us a probe — a personality "meter" you point at the activations.

The probes work — almost perfectly.

Held-out rank correlation between the probe's reading and the true trait, on characters it never saw during fitting. All five traits land between 0.93 and 0.98.

The five trait directions are also nearly perpendicular to each other (largest overlap |cos| = 0.11) — so "how agreeable" and "how conscientious" are read along genuinely separate axes, not one blurry blob. The directions live in the middle layers (around layer 30–36), where the model has moved past raw words into meaning.

What this really means. Personality isn't smeared randomly across the network. For each trait there's a clean, single line you can point at the model's "thoughts" and read off how much of that trait it's currently expressing — without asking it a thing.

A DETOUR · DID THE TRAITS FALL OUT ON THEIR OWN?

Do we even need the labels?

Fitting a probe uses the trait scores as an answer key. A fair question: could we skip the labels entirely and just ask what the biggest patterns of variation across characters are — using PCA, the standard "find the loudest directions" tool? (This is exactly how the Assistant Axis was discovered — unsupervised.) So we tried it here.

The answer is a revealing partly. Two traits pop straight out as the loudest axes — but the other three stay hidden.

Only PC1 (Conscientiousness) and PC2 (Agreeableness) light up. Extraversion, Emotional Stability and Openness are scattered across faint, lower axes — the unsupervised view simply misses them.

Why the two projects use different tools. PCA finds a persona axis instantly when the characters vary along one dominant theme — which is exactly the Assistant Axis's world (assistant vs. not). But Big Five is five themes, and three of them are quiet. So to get a clean dial for every trait you need the labels after all. Our steered dials are built from the supervised probes for precisely this reason — PCA alone would have handed us no usable Extraversion, Emotional-Stability, or Openness lever.

A neat rhyme: the single loudest personality axis across all 406 characters — competence / Conscientiousness — is also the trait that most closely describes the model's Assistant persona later on.

STEP 03 · STEERING

If a direction predicts a trait, add it to push the trait

A probe only reads. But the same direction is also a lever: while the model generates, we can nudge its activations along the trait line. Nudge toward the warm end for more of the trait, toward the cool end for less. The trick is how you push — a gentle nudge at one spot does little, but a firm, coordinated push across many layers moves the behaviour cleanly (without turning the output to gibberish).

Turn down Agreeableness and the helpful assistant becomes "don't cater to incompetence." Turn down Conscientiousness and its careful plan dissolves into procrastination.

STEP 04 · WHAT IT MOVES, AND WHAT LEAKS

The dial turns — but it isn't perfectly clean

Re-giving the model the full personality quiz while steering confirms the effect numerically. Pushing toward the cool end lowers the measured trait score every time — most dramatically for Agreeableness, which drops from 34.9 to 18.9 out of 50.

Measured trait score, default vs. steered-down

Bars: ■ default · ■ steered down. One quirk we found: with no persona at all, the model rates itself high on every trait — the default Assistant is agreeable, organized, calm and curious by nature — so there's more room to steer down than up.

Does steering one trait leave the others alone?

Only sometimes. When we steer each trait and watch all five, some dials are clean and some bleed into their neighbours. Agreeableness and Emotional Stability move in near-isolation; Openness is leaky — pushing it also drags Conscientiousness and Emotional Stability along. So the directions are separate when you read them, yet interfere when you push them.

A perfectly specific set of dials would light up only down the diagonal. Two of five do; Openness clearly doesn't.

STEP 05 · THE TWIST

Is "the helpful Assistant" just a Big Five profile?

There's a separately-discovered direction called the Assistant Axis — it measures how much like its trained, helpful default persona the model is being right now. With a Big Five basis in hand, we could finally ask: is being "the Assistant" simply a mix of high agreeableness, conscientiousness, and so on?

Mostly no. At the layers that matter, the Assistant Axis is almost perpendicular to every Big Five direction, and Big Five explains under half of what makes a character "Assistant-like" — leaving a 53% remainder orthogonal to human personality. Fittingly, the single biggest outlier — the persona that's far more "Assistant" than its Big Five profile predicts — is the assistant role itself.

An "AI-ness" that isn't on the human map. The helpful-Assistant persona isn't a point in personality space. It's a largely separate attractor — something the model became in training that human personality vocabulary can't fully name.

…but one human lever still moves it

Describing isn't the same as controlling. When we actually steer each trait and watch the Assistant Axis, four traits barely budge it — but Agreeableness is a powerful lever. Make the model disagreeable and it slides right off the Assistant persona:

◄ off the Assistant fully the Assistant ►
Assistant-Axis reading: default +1.63  →  agreeableness turned down −0.10

Notice the twist within the twist: the trait that best describes Assistant-ness on paper is Conscientiousness — but the trait that actually moves it is Agreeableness. What a direction correlates with, and what it causes, are not the same thing.

What we learned

Three places where reading, correlating, and causing come apart

Read ≠ Steer

A near-perfect meter is a mediocre lever

The same direction reads a trait at ρ≈0.95, yet needs a strong multi-layer push to steer — and the naïve nudge just breaks the output.

Separate ≠ Independent

Perpendicular directions still interfere

The five trait directions barely overlap in geometry, yet steering Openness drags other traits with it. Orthogonal to read, entangled to push.

Describe ≠ Cause

The best descriptor isn't the real lever

Conscientiousness best describes the Assistant; Agreeableness is what actually moves it off that persona.

CODA · WHERE THE PERSONAS ACTUALLY LAND

Reading the Big Five of the model's own personas

One more experiment closes the loop. The Assistant Axis is built from 275 personas the model can adopt — from helpful "assistant" and "consultant" to otherworldly "eldritch" and "void." We took the eight personas closest to the Assistant and the eight most drifted from it (plus the default), had the model answer 240 questions in each persona (1,200 responses each), and pointed our five Big Five probes at its responses. So each persona gets a measured Big Five profile — and we can ask what "drifting off the Assistant" is, in personality terms.

It has a remarkably clean signature. As personas drift away from the Assistant, they become colder, more withdrawn, less organized and less stable — but more imaginative.

Big Five profile: Assistant-like vs. drifted personas

■ Assistant-like (assistant, summarizer, consultant, instructor, planner, organizer, analyst, researcher) · ■ drifted (eldritch, leviathan, void, wraith, ghost, tree, vampire, absurdist)

Ranking all seventeen personas by how Assistant-like they are and correlating that with each trait tells the same story in one line — and it lands on the trait we already knew was the causal lever:

Correlation between a persona's Assistant-Axis position and its Big Five reading, across the seventeen personas.

Agreeableness is the through-line. The persona side, the steering side, and the profile side all agree: Agreeableness (r = +0.80) is what most closely tracks — and moves — the Assistant. Emotional Stability (+0.63), Extraversion (+0.55), and Conscientiousness (+0.40) all follow. Only Openness runs the opposite way (r = −0.44): the strange personas are the imaginative ones — "eldritch" scores the highest Openness of all, with the chaotic "absurdist" close behind.

An honest revision. On a smaller set of personas Conscientiousness looked decoupled from Assistant-ness — but with a wider net it clearly tracks (r = +0.40): the newly added professional personas ("organizer", "analyst", "researcher") are highly organized, while the drifted ones ("absurdist", "tree") are not. That pulls this response-level readout back into partial agreement with the earlier role-vector analysis, which had also leaned on Conscientiousness — while Agreeableness stays the strongest signal throughout, and the causal test still sides with it. One thing that didn't change: the personas are no more erratic than the Assistant ones (within a single persona the Big Five readings vary about equally — drifted, if anything, a touch more consistent). Drifting changes which personality the model has, not how steadily it holds it.

EXPANSION · WHO vs. WHAT

Is the model's persona about who the user is, or what's happening?

We held the user fixed and escalated the situation from normal to extreme across five modes, then split the variance in the model's persona into a user effect and a scenario effect.

What decides the model's persona?

■ user (who) · ■ scenario (what) · ■ interaction · ■ noise

How Assistant-like the model is comes almost entirely from who the user is (72%), barely from the situation (8%). But Extraversion and Openness genuinely flex with the situation — those are the traits a scenario moves.

Cut 1 — same user, escalate the situation

Fix the user (a generic, no-persona user) and turn one emotional-distress situation from normal to extreme. The persona deepens: counselor → caregiver.

But the direction depends on the mode. Escalate a high-stakes decision instead and the model slides the other way — recruiter → coach → counselor, Assistant-Axis falling +2.29 → +1.57 as the deliberative side takes over; a moral dilemma crashes it further still (0.64 → 0.13).

Cut 2 — same message, different user

The sharper test: freeze the exact same high-stakes-extreme message and only change who is asking. One identical request fans out across an Assistant-Axis range of 2.6 — a warm counselor for one user, a fully drifted in-character judge for another.

Same words in, radically different persona out — the model reads the user off their standing profile before the situation even lands.

Who beats what — but an emotional extreme can override it. By default the user's disposition dominates (Assistant-likeness is 72% who, 8% what). Yet an acute emotional situation can flip it: a cool expert user, pushed into extreme distress, warms up and is pulled back into caregiver mode (Agreeableness +0.10→+0.20). The override is specific to emotional need — that same expert in an extreme ethical dilemma stays a cool mathematician. Intensity alone doesn't do it; the kind of intensity does.

Under the hood. All results come from meta-llama/Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct. Probes are ridge-regression directions on residual-stream activations over 406 characters; steering adds those directions back during generation; the Assistant-Axis analysis reuses published axis vectors. Every number here is measured, and every steered quote is a real, unedited generation.
